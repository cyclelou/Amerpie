> "Things of quality have no fear of time."

When I posted that first, anonymous quote to my Facebook page [11 years ago](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://www.facebook.com/4minutebooks/photos/pb.100069513504238.-2207520000/393208667442454/), I had no idea I would become a writer, let alone one who depends on lines like this for a living.

Since then, I have written two academic theses, [two books](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://nik.art/books), and over 2,000 individual articles. More than 500 of those pieces form the backbone of [Four Minute Books](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://fourminutebooks.com/summaries), a collection of over 1,000 short but high-quality book summaries that has attracted over 18 million readers to date.

Across all of those projects, I've been what I was raised to be: a stickler for the details. It might be my German roots, my mom's talent for keeping an impeccable household that rubbed off on me, or my introvert tendency to spend most of my time observing, but when I read something, I'm hell-bent on the content matching the label on the box.

The filmmaker Kirby Ferguson says that "[everything is a remix](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://youtu.be/nJPERZDfyWc)," and given it is one of the oldest forms of art, perhaps writing especially both benefits and suffers from the fact that "there is nothing new under the sun," a line which, ironically, goes back all the way to the Bible — [the Book of Ecclesiastes](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://biblehub.com/ecclesiastes/1-9.htm), to be exact.

Since writing is both prone to repetition and largely dependent on it, one of the best ways to make your writing bulletproof is to not just actively check your writing for lines that may have been written before but to actively incorporate existing ideas and then give appropriate credit to the authors who originated them.

As readers, we don't like writers who rip off other writers, even inadvertently, but we appreciate writers who can layer many versions of the same idea on top of one another to drive a point home — even more so if they are generous in pointing to where they got those many layers from. As a writer, I'm trying my best to be in the latter camp, but this journey is not without obstacles.

Assuming I've quoted only once per piece I've written — a conservative estimate — that's 2,000 times I've quoted someone in the last ten years. This means I've quoted far more than most people and even most writers, and along the way, I've learned a thing or two. Perhaps the most pertinent lesson? When you do something frequently, you're bound to get it wrong from time to time. [This quote list](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://nik.art/comfort-zone-quotes/) I wrote in 2015, for example, still contains several lines I can't find proper sources for to this day.

On Four Minute Books, every book summary includes at least one quote in image-form, and though I haven't written all the summaries, it has been my job to verify all the quotes as authentic. Sometimes, authors quote other authors in their books, and sometimes, those quotes become the most popular on sites like Goodreads, where the original attribution may get lost.

On [our summary of *The Daily Stoic*](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://fourminutebooks.com/the-daily-stoic-summary/) by Ryan Holiday, for example, the writer accidentally chose the famous "[serenity prayer](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serenity_Prayer)" as the featured quote, which Holiday cites correctly in the book but [Goodreads doesn’t](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/49328756-the-daily-stoic-366-meditations-on-wisdom-perseverance-and-the-art-of). Ironically, the prayer's origins are somewhat murky to begin with, with theologian Reinhold Niebuhr as the most likely but never undisputed source.

Misattributions and errors in sourcing are as old as the written word itself, but with the rise of the internet, it became almost cool to gloss over them. If you and I had a dollar for each fake quote floating around the web, neither of us would have to work ever again. And though it appears the tide is slowly shifting, another lesson I've learned is that it's one thing to identify and avoid bad information online, but to actively fight it is another.

Especially since the 2016 US election and later the pandemic, it has become en vogue to yell "fake news" and "misinformation" at every piece of journalism we don't like, but I find few of the slacktivists throwing around these terms then proceed to actively dig for better sources, let alone share those sources with their community. Quotes seem to be hit especially hard in this regard.

For example, on Goodreads, possibly the 1 destination where internet searchers land while looking for quotes, 7 of [the top 10 most popular quotes](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://www.goodreads.com/quotes) are misattributions. Some of these are actually tagged as such, but what do people see and remember when they browse such pages? Not the fine print but Albert Einstein's picture next to some words he purportedly said.

Many other big quote websites, like BrainyQuote, don't bother tagging quotes at all. They conveniently offer a "Cite" link for you to use the quote as gospel, but they don't bother verifying if the quote itself is actually correct. When in doubt, just [file it under Mark Twain](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://www.brainyquote.com/authors/mark-twain-quotes) and move on.

Google compounds this issue by constantly ranking websites highly for keywords they have no business ranking for. When it comes to "famous quotes" in particular, Hubspot, a company making marketing software — clearly a stalwart of literature — has been the 1 result for a long time. Naturally, their results page is not only littered with CTAs and irrelevant embeds, it also starts with not just one but two misattributions right off the bat. Other high-ranking results come from Adobe, Indeed, and other non-literary websites clearly abusing their authority, like Wavebox ranking Lady Gaga at the very top of [its list](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://wavebox.io/knowhow/the-50-most-famous-quotes-of-all-time/) of "most famous quotes of all time." Gaga indeed.

Eventually, while digging into this mess last year, I realized that when it comes to quotes and their sources, the same rule holds up that applies pretty much everywhere else in life: No one cares as much as you do, and if you don't do something, no one else will. That's when I decided to start what would become a many-week project of compiling the *actually* most famous quotes of all time — with their real authors, of course.

I need good ideas for my creativity. I need good quotes for my writing. And I need those quotes to be authentic for my reputation. I also don't believe we spent thousands of years of documenting human ingenuity only to now listen to robots telling us what came from where and then taking what they say at face value. Therefore, after several hundred hours of research and verification, I present this personal study turned minor PhD thesis: [*The 365 Most Famous Quotes of All Time*](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://fourminutebooks.com/famous-quotes/).

Before we get into the weeds of this Moby Dick of a post, let's clarify a few things: What exactly did I make, how did I go about it, and how can you best make yourself at home in this tome?

## How Did You Decide Which Quotes To Include?

As we've seen, documenting quotes and tracking their popularity are "two pairs of shoes," as we say in Germany, and for all our tech, we struggle with both of them. The fact that "famous" is a relative term doesn't help either.

Thankfully, there *are* ways we can measure popularity objectively, and even if we can't execute those measurements perfectly, combining data and common sense will go a long way in making good approximations. That's what I tried to do in this study.

The most famous quote in the world would be a line all 8 billion people on this planet know. Therefore, judging any quote's popularity becomes an estimation question: How many of those 8 billion folks can you reasonably assume to know this line? I started my guesses with two questions:

1. **If I approached random people in the street, would they recognize this quote *and* know its author?**
2. **If they knew the quote but not the author off the top of their head, would they be likely to guess the correct author if given 3 options?**

Then, I asked some additional questions:

- **Is this a quote that's common in many languages?** Can I find various translations of it?
- **How likely is it that not just thousands but *millions* of people have heard this quote?** Since [some 650 million people](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://www.nasa.gov/mission_pages/apollo/missions/apollo11.html) watched or listened to the moon landing in 1969, it doesn't take a rocket scientist to understand that Neil Armstrong's, "One small step for man, one giant leap for mankind," is rather popular.
- **Is this a quote people search for on a regular basis?** To verify this, I checked the [Google search volume](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://surferseo.com/) of various versions of the quote, [Google Trends](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://trends.google.com/trends/), and the number of votes for the quote on [Goodreads](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://goodreads.com/).

Finally, to authenticate each quote's source, I used the following resources, among others:

- [**Quote Investigator**](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://quoteinvestigator.com/)
- [**Wikiquote**](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://www.wikiquote.org/)
- [**Project Gutenberg**](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://gutenberg.org/)
- [**The Internet Archive**](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://archive.org/)
- [**Sue Brewton**](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://suebrewton.com/)
- **Time's ranking of "**[***The 100 Most Significant Figures in History***](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://ideas.time.com/2013/12/10/whos-biggest-the-100-most-significant-figures-in-history/)***"***

For more details on my methodology for analysis and verification, see [this section at the end](https://archive.ph/PCsft#c957).

## The 10 Most Famous Quotes Overall

![[IMG-20250401103625879-The 365 Most Famous Quotes of All Time.webp]]

Image created with [Pablo](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://pablo.buffer.com/), background image via [Unsplash](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://unsplash.com/photos/black-and-white-photo-lot-Fo5dTm6ID1Y)

**What are the 10 most popular phrases in the history of mankind?**

This is as tough of a question as it gets. Here's my attempted answer: If we're considering everyday use, I believe all of the most famous quotes would be proverbs. "An apple a day keeps the doctor away," and so on. But hardly anyone knows their sources, so I went with a different approach:

**What are the *most* famous quotes from the *most* famous people in the *most* famous categories?**

I used Google search volume, reported data, and other indicators (if a phrase has its own Wikipedia entry, that's a good sign, for example) to pick what I think are the most recognizable quotes in 10 categories: religion, military leaders, ancient and modern philosophy, famous poets and poems, great novels and fictional characters, as well as sports and explorers.

**These quotes would be at the top of their respective categories, but since we won't do double-mentions, I've grouped them together in this overall category that takes the crown.** For details, see the source notes at the very end of this post.

Here are the 10 most famous quotes of all time:

The book review and tracking platform Goodreads has [over 125 million members](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://www.goodreads.com/advertisers). Therefore, when it comes to famous quotes from books and writers but also celebrities in general, they have a great amount of data available. As you might expect, the results here skew towards people who love books and reading. Still, their top 10 quotes of all time aren't *just* from writers. The list includes scientists, politicians, comedians, and civil rights icons. It is also, however, riddled with misattributions.

**Of the top 10 most liked quotes on Goodreads, 7 are misattributed, 2 have shaky sourcing (Einstein and Gandhi), and only 1 is correct — and even that line has been flipped upside down (Cicero said that books *gave* his house a soul, not the other way around).**

None of that has hurt their popularity. In fact, it has likely helped. We love to believe we're quoting Einstein, Marilyn Monroe, or Gandhi, and even when that's not actually the case, the ideas are still great.

**Each of the following quotes has received over 100,000 likes on Goodreads.** Only 2 other quotes outside of the top 10 meet this criterion. You'll find both of those elsewhere on this list.

## Top 14 Most Popular Quotes From Famous Modern Philosophers (Post-15th Century)

![[IMG-20250401103644171-The 365 Most Famous Quotes of All Time.webp]]

Image created with [Pablo](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://pablo.buffer.com/), background image via [Unsplash](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://unsplash.com/photos/woman-in-dress-statue-in-grayscale-photography-wZfndoCA1rA)

After an early peak in intellectual activity, the advancement of human thought [went dark for almost 1,500 years](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_Ages_(historiography)), at least in the West. From the year 0 all the way past the Middle Ages, philosophy (and civilization in general) had little to show for.

That all changed with the Renaissance and later the Enlightenment period, starting from around 1300 AD in Italy, particularly Florence. It was a time of reason, art, philosophy, culture, and technology. With it came countless intellectual breakthroughs.

## What Really Makes a Quote "Famous?" (Methodology)

Quotes have been popular since well before the internet. As early as 600 BC, ancient Greek storyteller [Aesop](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesop) wrote [dozens of fables](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Aesop's_Fables), many of which came with pithy lessons that [survive to this day](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesop's_Fables). In 1500, the Dutch philosopher [Erasmus](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erasmus) compiled over 4,000 Latin and Greek sayings in his [*Adagia*](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adagia), a collection of proverbs and sayings.

Given how long we've been documenting quotes, I thought a quick Google search would help me find out exactly how popular each particular line is. As it turns out, however, documenting quotes and tracking their popularity are two different things — and most people do neither very well.

Of course, "famous" is a subjective term. What's popular varies wildly from person to person, country to country, and even day to day. But if I've never even *heard* of the first 8 quotes in the top 10 of some list, clearly, none of those lines are not actually all that famous.

But there are some objective measures of popularity, and wherever those measures failed me, I tried to approximate well.

## Analysis

Generally, there are two reasons why a quote might be popular:

1. It's a line many people know
2. It was said by *a person* many people know

Ideally, both are true. Often, however, we'll know the words but not the originator or vice versa. Therefore, I used the following two questions to pick the quotes for this list:

1. **If I approached random people in the street, would they recognize this quote *and* know its author?**
2. **If they knew the quote but not the author off the top of their head, would they be likely to guess the correct author if given 3 options?**

For example, you probably know the phrase "I came, I saw, I conquered." And you probably also know that it came from Julius Caesar. A quote like that qualifies as per the first question.

Similarly, you might not have heard the exact phrase, "Possessions make you rich? I don't have that type of richness. My richness is life, forever." But if I gave you three choices, say, Elvis Presley, Bob Marley, and Céline Dion, you can probably guess that this line came from Bob Marley. This quote qualifies as per the second question.

I also asked myself further questions to decide which quotes to pick:

- **Is this a quote that's been translated into many languages?** My native tongue is German, but I also know a bit of Latin, French and Spanish. So, while far from perfect and with a little help from Google Translate, I could at least tell for some quotes whether they'd made it beyond the English language. I can't speak for Asian or African languages, of course, but I imagine even in China, a lot of people will know a few lines from Shakespeare, for example.
- **Have millions of people heard this quote?** Martin Luther King Jr.'s speech "I have a dream," was not only heard live by 250,000 people. It was also broadcast widely via television and the radio. The chances that millions of people know this quote are quite high.
- **Is this a quote people search for on a regular basis?** Wherever possible, I used [Google search volume](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://surferseo.com/), [Google Trends](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://trends.google.com/trends/), and votes from [Goodreads](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://goodreads.com/)' 125 million members to determine whether one quote is more popular than another. This also helped in verifying if a quote is actually that author's most famous line. [Yoda’s most popular quote](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://fourminutebooks.com/yoda-quotes/%23The_10_Most_Popular_Quotes_From_Yoda) ("Do. Or do not. There is no try."), for example, gets more search requests than "Yoda quotes" altogether. It seems more people remember this quote than who it came from in the first place. That makes it a strong contender for being his most popular phrase.

## Verification

Nowadays, there are more misattributed quotes flying around the internet than mosquitoes in the summer. I'm not a full-time academic, but I did get both a Bachelor's and a Master's degree. In both, we learned how to properly vet, source, and attribute information.

**Therefore, you'll find a full, dedicated "Sources" category with links to where exactly each quote is from right below this section.** Where possible, I tried to link to the original source. For old books that are freely available online, I've linked to the relevant section in the text. For newer books, I've linked to their Goodreads page. Feel free to borrow them from your local library and double-check! Where direct sources weren't available, I've linked to whoever did the hard work of vetting the line in question.

The following resources have been extremely helpful in verifying all these quotes:

- [**Quote Investigator**](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://quoteinvestigator.com/). Garson O'Toole is the 1 quote sleuth on the web. Thank you, Sir! Read his articles where linked for best-in-class tracking of where a line originally came from.
- [**Wikiquote**](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://www.wikiquote.org/). The collective magic of Wikipedia contributors also works when applied to quotes. Amazing. Wherever I've linked Wikiquote, you can search on the Wikiquote page to find the exact source of the quote in question.
- [**Project Gutenberg**](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://gutenberg.org/). If it's an important work of literature, you'll likely find it here. Over 70,000 full, free books, mostly older, historically important works that are in the public domain. And so easy to search, too. Fantastic!
- [**The Internet Archive**](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://archive.org/). From full-text books to other helpful, rare resources, this non-profit website truly is a treasure gem for any researcher.
- [**Sue Brewton**](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://suebrewton.com/). A self-appointed "quotologist," I landed on Sue's website more than once in my research. Thank you!
- [***The 100 Most Significant Figures in History***](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://ideas.time.com/2013/12/10/whos-biggest-the-100-most-significant-figures-in-history/). This statistical ranking, created by Time Magazine, helped a lot in determining who deserved to be on this list, and who might just be a passing trend on social media. When thinking of famous people, we tend to over-index on whoever's popular right now. I hate to say it, but compared with Napoleon and Joan of Arc, Johnny Depp and Taylor Swift will likely be nothing more than a blip on history's radar. Whoever has 500, 1,000, even 2,000 years of significance under their belt already is likely to persist just as long into the future.

Since a good number of the quotes on this list ended up being from German-speaking sources (or at least also prevalent in their German translation), I double-checked German sources wherever possible. "Stitched twice holds better," we say in German. 😉

**Finally, and perhaps above all, I used my brain and common sense.** I've been reading books for over 25 years, and while I've never won "Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?" I'm a pretty decent "quiz whiz." I have a broad stock of common knowledge, not least thanks to summarizing hundreds of books on [Four Minute Books](https://archive.ph/o/PCsft/https://fourminutebooks.com/book-summaries/).

If a quote sounds straight out of a 2000s high school movie but is ascribed to someone born in the 18th century, chances are, something's not right. It's often worth trusting our gut if we're skeptical, and even just with a laptop and an internet connection, it's surprising how much you can find out if you keep digging.

**Oh, and each quote is only listed once, of course.** A rare handful of authors makes more than one appearance (this Shakespeare guy was just too good), but all quotes are unique.

So much for the methodology. I hope you find it reasonable.
